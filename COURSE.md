# Teaching with this template

I am building a class on human-shaped software around this repository:
apps built with an AI agent for human-shaped problems, that leave people
more capable rather than more passive. The cohorts run from
humanshaped.org, and they are free. Anyone can teach one. I have not
taught it yet. This page is the shape I expect it to take, written down
so that I can be wrong about it in specific ways, and it has already
changed once, after I asked what the research on teaching small online
groups had to say about my first draft.

## A class about learning, three ways

The class is about learning before it is about apps. Each student is
learning in three ways at once, and each week asks something of all
three.

- **As a student,** they learn a way of building by doing it and then
  showing it to other people. Nobody learns this method by reading it.
- **As a builder,** they learn what their app is. The agent writes the
  code. Deciding, judging and using the app are theirs, and the class
  protects that work rather than rushing past it.
- **As a human,** they decide what to hand to the agent and what to
  keep, and they build something that does the same for the people who
  use it.

Every week makes the same three moves as the stages: write, play, and
publish. Stage 00 explains them.

make.

## The one requirement

Every student builds one real app of their own choosing, for a
human-shaped problem and for people they can actually name. Not a
tutorial app, but something they want to exist, and every week's work is
done on that app. A human-shaped problem is one they have lived with,
in my words "something that has gone unsolved for months or even years,"
and "something that you cannot do yourself" with the tools and resources
they have now
([my definition, and examples of each shape](docs/human-shaped/computer-shaped-problems.md#computer-shaped-and-human-shaped)).
Nobody arrives with the app already chosen.

## Five weeks, and a week before them

Each week the same app gets further along, and each week's work is a
set of conversations with the agent, not a set of files to edit. Two
stages share most weeks, so the pace is quick. That is on purpose: the
stages are built to be lapped more than once, and a cohort is the first
lap, done with other people.

Five weeks gets most people to a shared, working app on one or two
platforms. Store listings and the later stages often land after the
cohort ends, and that is fine. Nobody graduates out of the community,
and the app's page stays open while it keeps growing.

## Cohort Prep

The week before week 1 is for getting set up and for meeting the people
you will build beside, because nobody should be asked to say what they
think of someone's work before they know who is in the room. [Getting
set up](docs/path/setup.md) takes each student from no GitHub account to
their own app's address on their phone, and the setup session is where
anything still stuck gets unstuck, in front of people who will need the
same answer. It is also when each student starts a five-day noticing
log, one line whenever they wished something worked differently, gave up
on something they care about, did something the long way because nothing
helped, or saw someone they know struggle with the same thing, so that
week 1 begins with real material to choose from
([stage 00, Notice before you choose](docs/path/00-why-we-build.md#notice-before-you-choose)).

It is also when each student meets their group and hears three things
said aloud. The first is the four rules below for how we talk to one
another. The second is one true story about how rough a first week is.
Mine is Archive Watch's third day, when the commits run from "UI
feedback pass" to "UI feedback round 7", each round a list of what was
still wrong, and I want everyone to know from the start that a long list
of what is wrong is what the method looks like when it is working.

The third is what a bring-back looks like before anyone has to make
one. The teacher shows a strong bring-back and a weak one, side by side,
and everyone practices the three questions from [Showing your
work](docs/path/showing-your-work.md) on the weak one, once, so that the
first time anyone answers them about a classmate's app, they have
already done it about an app that belongs to nobody.

## Four rules for how we talk to one another

These are said aloud in the week before, and again whenever one of them
is broken. They are not a pledge anyone signs. They are a short list of
the small things that make people stop showing unfinished work, and
everyone in the cohort, including the teacher, will break one sooner or
later. When that happens, someone says so plainly, the person who broke
it says "thanks," and we move on.

1. **No correcting what does not matter.** If what someone said is
   close enough for what they were saying, let it stand.
2. **No acting surprised.** Nobody says "you don't know what a
   repository is?" Everyone here is learning something they did not
   know last week.
3. **No taking over.** When someone is working out what to say to their
   agent, do not tell them what to type unless they ask. The deciding is
   the part they came to learn.
4. **No small digs at who someone is.** No jokes or asides about
   anyone's age, background, gender, body, accent, or where they are
   from, even ones meant kindly.

## Groups of three that stay together

Each student builds their own app and shows it in a group of three, kept
together across all five weeks, so that the people who see your work
have seen last week's version too. I want three rather than two because
a group's job here is to see one another's apps and say something
useful about them, and a pair has nobody to show when one person is
away. When the numbers do not divide by three, a pair joins another
pair for the bring-back rather than anyone showing alone.

Groups are visible, so every student knows who their partners are and
what the partnership expects of them:

- Each week, see one another's "Be ready to..." work on a device, hear
  one decision explained, and answer the three questions in [Showing
  your work](docs/path/showing-your-work.md).
- Between sessions, open one another's app on your own device and look
  at what changed.
- When a partner goes quiet, reach out to them first, kindly, and then
  let the teacher know.

At the end of week 2 the teacher asks each person, privately, whether
their group is working for them, and changes any group that is not. In
week 3, everyone also opens two apps from outside their group and
leaves the three questions in writing, which is the one look across
groups before the final showing.

The cost of a stable group is that you see fewer of your classmates
closely. The week 3 look and week 5's showing, open to everyone, are
where that evens out.

## The six parts of a session

Each live session runs about 75 minutes, in the same six parts every
week, so that nobody spends the first ten minutes wondering what
happens next. The [weekly guides](docs/teaching/README.md) give each
part its minutes, and say how to fit the session into 60 or 90.

1. **Arrive.** Everyone writes one line in the chat as they come in:
   what they shipped this week, or where they are stuck. Then the
   teacher says, in a minute or two, what they read in last week's
   checks for understanding and what they changed because of it. That
   minute is what makes the check worth answering. A student who wrote
   that they are stuck gets asked, in the chat, whether they would like
   theirs to be the stuck moment the group works on, or to stay a few
   minutes after. Nobody is named out loud for being stuck.
2. **Show what you brought back.** In their groups of three, about eight
   minutes each, someone keeping time. Each student says in one line
   what they want to know from their partners, shows their "Be ready
   to..." work on the device it runs on, and explains one decision
   behind it with the agent closed (a decision, never the code). Their
   partners ask one question to understand it better before saying
   anything about it, and "what did you give up?" is a good one. Then
   the partners answer three questions while the student listens: where
   is it going, how is it going, and what is next. The student ends by
   saying what they will do next, and whether the stage is ready or not
   yet, which the next section explains. The teacher visits the groups,
   says so on arriving so that nobody stops to perform, and listens for
   one thing that comes up in more than one group.
3. **One value at work.** Back together, one person shares a decision
   their values changed that week, and what it cost them. Then the
   teacher names the one thing they heard across the groups. This part
   gets its own few minutes, after the groups rather than inside them,
   because a value said to the whole cohort is a value other people can
   borrow.
4. **Read one real prompt together, trying it first.** The teacher puts
   up a real situation (the screenshot, the bug, what the agent said)
   without the prompt that answered it. Everyone writes the prompt they
   would send, on their own, then compares it with a partner, and only
   then does the real prompt go up, so the conversation is about the
   difference: what the builder wanted, what the agent got wrong, and
   how the prompt fixed it. For the deeper part of a skill, trying first
   and comparing after teaches more than seeing the answer first. The
   situations move toward the cohort's own work as the weeks go on. In
   the first two weeks they come from the stages. In weeks 3 and 4 they
   come from a classmate's real stuck moment, shared with their
   permission. In week 5, someone who wants to leads with a moment of
   their own. Nobody is required to lead.
5. **Start.** Everyone writes the first prompt of the coming week and
   sends it to their agent before they leave, with the four questions on
   the wall and a room open for anyone who gets stuck. I want everyone
   to walk away from the session with the week already begun, rather
   than with a plan to begin it.
6. **Check for understanding.** In the last three minutes, every student
   answers two questions privately, on the cohort's live page or in a
   message only the teacher sees, and never in the shared chat, where
   the first answer shapes all the others. The first is the week's
   question, drawn from its stages, such as "What can you decide or judge
   about your app now that you could not last week?" The second is
   always the same: "What is still muddy?" The teacher reads every
   answer before the next session, because those lines are how a teacher
   finds out who understood the week and who needs a word, and then
   opens the next session by saying what they heard.

Showing and explaining are not saved for the live session. Every
stage's "Be ready to..." line asks for something to show and a decision
to explain, so a student who walks a partner through a decision
mid-week, or posts a round in the cohort's conversation, is doing the
same work between sessions.

## Ready, or not yet

Every stage has a paragraph that begins "When you are ready to move
on." That paragraph is the bar, and the only one. There are no grades,
no points, and no partial credit, only two answers: ready, or not yet.

**The builder judges first.** Before the session, each student reads the
stage's paragraph and decides, for each thing it names, whether their
app is there. A partner confirms by seeing it on a device, not by
hearing about it, because "it works" is a claim and a phone in
someone's hand is evidence.

**"Not yet" says what is missing, and costs nothing.** Doing it again is
free, and there is no deadline to miss. The cohort keeps its weekly
rhythm either way, so a "not yet" travels with you into next week rather
than holding you back from it.

**The teacher looks closely at the moments that matter most.** That
means week 4's session, when the app is first shown on someone else's
device, and week 5, at the final showing. It also means any time someone says "not
yet" about the same stage for the second time, because by then a
conversation is worth more than another round alone.

The cost is that people who are new to building are judging work in a
field they are new to. Seeing the app on a device does most of that
work, and the strong and weak bring-backs from the week before do some
of the rest.

## When someone goes quiet

A partner who notices first reaches out first, kindly. The teacher does
too: within two days of a missed session, or a week with no bring-back,
the teacher writes to that person privately, with an easy way back in
and an easy way out. Life happens in five weeks, and leaving a cohort
should never feel like failing it. The recording and the stages are both
there when someone comes back, and so is their group.

## Cameras and breaks

These are written down so nobody has to guess, and the teacher says them
at the start of the first session.

- **Cameras are welcome, and never required.** Video calls tire people,
  and not equally, so a camera is a choice and not a measure of
  attention. Nobody's participation is ever counted by their camera.
- There are other ways to be in the room: a line in the chat, a raised
  hand, your phone's screen shared, and your answers to the check.
- Cameras do the most good while showing and while talking in a small
  group. During the start of the week's work, off is fine, and hiding
  your own picture helps.
- Show the app by sharing your phone's screen, which puts everyone's
  eyes on the work rather than on a face.
- Any session longer than an hour has a short break near its middle.

The cost is that a teacher sees less of the room. The check for
understanding at the end of each session carries what a face would have
said.

## A value is worth what it changed

A list of values can feel like doing the work without being the work.
People who study ethics toolkits have found exactly that: teams fill in
the worksheet and nothing about the product changes. So, the test I
want students to use on themselves is a plain one.

**A value that never changed a decision is decoration.**

That is why "one value at work" asks for a decision, not a value. "I
care about privacy" is a value. "I cut the sign-in screen, because
nothing in the app needed to know who you were" is a value at work.

## Feedback from your own agent

Each student can also ask their own agent for a human-shaped review.
It asks the questions this method asks, it says it is an AI, and it
never stands in for the teacher's feedback or a partner's. One small
study found that people take an AI's feedback more readily than a
teacher's and improve from it the least, and a larger review found no
difference on average, so I would rather not lean on either. The course
asks one thing before anyone acts on an AI review: write down, in your
note, what you agree with and what you do not, and why. [Showing your
work](docs/path/showing-your-work.md) has the step.

## Teaching a cohort of your own

Anyone can teach a cohort, and nobody needs to be an expert in building
apps to do it, because the teacher's job is to run the session, read
the checks, and notice who needs a word. I want a new teacher's first
session to feel familiar, so they co-lead one session of someone
else's cohort first, taking the parts they are most comfortable with,
and then run their own from the [weekly guides](docs/teaching/README.md).
Each guide is a session on a page: the minutes, the words for the
opening and the close, the check question, what to look for in the
bring-backs, the prompt situation, and what to do afterward.

## What a cohort gives back

Every app I built folded its lessons back into this template, which is
why it is on its sixth generation (`docs/PROVENANCE.md` records where
each lesson came from). A cohort is one more source of lessons. In week
5, the cohort chooses together one thing it learned that the next
builder should not have to learn again (a prompt that worked, a step
that broke, a correction that kept coming up) and sends it back to the
template as an issue or a pull request, with the people who learned it
credited. It is one lesson from the whole cohort, never a requirement
for any one person.

## Without a cohort

Most people who build from this template will do it on their own, and
the same feedback is within reach for them: a friend as a partner, the
human-shaped review as AI feedback labeled as AI, and a public post
where the app's people already are. [Showing your
work](docs/path/showing-your-work.md) says how.

## What I am unsure about

The research answered two of my doubts well enough to choose, and left
the rest open.

- **Pairs or trios.** I chose trios, for the reason above: a trio still
  has two people to show when one is away. I could not find a study
  that compares the two for adults building their own projects online,
  so this is reasoning rather than evidence, and I want to learn whether
  it holds.
- **How fast to hand over the prompt reading.** Showing a lot at first
  and less each week is what the research recommends, and five weeks is
  a short time to go from my prompts to theirs. So, the handover now
  runs through the cohort's own stuck moments rather than asking
  everyone to lead by week 5. What I still do not know is whether
  anyone will want to lead in week 5, and the session works if nobody
  does.
- **Whether partners can judge "ready."** Seeing the app on a device
  answers most of it. I do not yet know how well people who are new to
  building can judge the rest of a stage's bar for one another.
- **Five weeks is fast** for people who have never used a terminal or an
  AI agent. Cohort Prep exists for that reason, and some people
  will need two laps of the stages rather than one.
- **Store accounts cost money** ($99 a year for Apple, $25 once for
  Google). Week 3 ends at an app installed on someone else's device,
  which a direct share can do for free on Android and the web version
  can do on an iPhone (test tracks need the store accounts). Android's
  free limited-distribution accounts, for students, teachers, and
  hobbyists, share an app with up to 20 devices (stage 05, "Sharing it
  for free").
- **How much to leave to discovery.** Research on teaching beginners
  is clear that open exploration without guidance leaves many of them
  lost. The method gives every stage numbered steps for that reason. I do
  not yet know how much room to leave inside them for students to find
  their own way.
- **Real devices are unevenly distributed.** Some students own three,
  some own one. Stage 04 is written so that one phone is enough, and I
  want to test that promise.

## Where these came from

Most of the moves on this page come from people who have run small
groups of learners for a long time, restated here in this course's
words. The opening check-in and the weekly facilitator guide come from
P2PU's learning circles. Reading every check and saying what changed
comes from The Carpentries' minute cards and Stephen Brookfield's
Critical Incident Questionnaire, and a new teacher co-leading before
leading alone comes from The Carpentries too. Saying what you want to know, and asking a
clarifying question before giving an opinion, come from the Tuning
protocol and Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process. The three
questions are John Hattie and Helen Timperley's model of feedback.
"Ready or not yet" borrows from specifications grading, without the
grades. Trying the prompt before seeing it rests on Peer Instruction
and productive failure, and sending the first prompt before leaving
rests on the research on implementation intentions. The four rules are
adapted from the Recurse Center's social rules, and so is reaching out
when someone goes quiet. The research notes behind every change, with
which sources were checked and which were cited from memory, are in
[humanshaped.org's research
folder](https://github.com/bhwilkoff/UniversalAppTemplate/blob/site/research/notes/facilitation-assessment-social-learning-notes.md).

If you teach with this before I do, I would love to hear what broke.
