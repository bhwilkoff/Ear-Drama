# I cannot find a problem worth building for

*Written by Claude, awaiting Ben's review. Rebuilt from
[curriculum research note 06](../../research/curriculum/06-instructional-design.md),
October 7, 2026.*

Not finding the problem on the first try is normal. None of my four apps
began as an idea for an app, and how I chose each problem over others
happened in my head, where no commit could record it
([research note 01](../../research/curriculum/01-mining-bens-process.md#what-the-record-cannot-teach)).
Research on problem finding points the same way: people who spend longer
exploring before they commit tend to make more original work (Getzels
and Csikszentmihalyi, *The Creative Vision*, 1976, as summarized in
[note 06](../../research/curriculum/06-instructional-design.md)). Taking
longer here is not falling behind.

**Ask yourself first.** Which line in my noticing log would I most like
to never have to write again?

**Try this.**

1. **Go back to the log, not to your head.** Ideas come from noticing,
   not from thinking one up on the spot (Paul Graham,
   [How to Get Startup Ideas](http://www.paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)).
   If your log is thin, keep it two more days with the same four prompts
   from [stage 00](../../path/00-why-we-build.md#notice-before-you-choose):
   I wished something worked differently, I gave up on something I care
   about, I did something the long way because nothing helped, someone I
   know struggled with the same thing.
2. **Hold what you have to the ten tests.** Put every line from the log
   beside [the ten tests](../../path/00-why-we-build.md#ten-things-to-hold-a-problem-to)
   and mark them "yes," "not yet," or "I don't know." A candidate with
   several "I don't know" marks is not a weak one. It is one you have not
   asked anyone about yet.
3. **Let a person, not an agent, help you choose.** Pick the two lines
   you keep coming back to and ask someone in your life, "Tell me about
   the last time this happened to you." The one they have a story about
   is usually worth more than the one you can argue for.
4. **If you want the agent's help, use it as a listener.** Keep it from
   writing your problem for you:

   > Here are some lines from my notes about things that went wrong this
   > week. Do not suggest apps, and do not rewrite them. For each one,
   > ask me one question about how long it has been going on and who else
   > it happens to.

   Answer its questions in your own words, in your note.

**From my apps.** None of my four apps began as an idea for an app, and
for each one I wrote the why and did the research before building
([research note 01](../../research/curriculum/01-mining-bens-process.md),
phases 1 and 2). Archive Watch began on an evening in April, as an app
"for watching old public-domain films on an Apple TV"
([the case study](../../human-shaped/case-study-archive-watch.md)).

**In any tool.** This page is the same in Claude Code, Antigravity, and
the open path, because the work is your own noticing and your own
conversations. The agent, if you use it at all, only asks questions.

**Go deeper.** Read my
[four human-shaped problem statements](../../human-shaped/problem-statements.md)
and write down which one is closest in kind to the line you keep coming
back to, and why. Then write your line in the same form.

**Who to ask.** Your trio first, because they have heard you talk about
your week and often notice the line before you do. Then your teacher,
because choosing the problem is a decision no agent can make for you.

A log, ten tests, one conversation, and then a choice.
