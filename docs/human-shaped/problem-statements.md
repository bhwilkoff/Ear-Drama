In October I wrote down four problems I would call human-shaped, and four
I would call computer-shaped, in
[computer-shaped problems](computer-shaped-problems.md#computer-shaped-and-human-shaped).
Which is which is settled, so this page does not ask you to sort them.
It asks a different question, one only you can answer about your own
work:

**What makes a problem statement strong enough to build from?**

You are going to write one for your own problem, so you study these the
way a writer studies a good paragraph before writing their own. Ron
Berger calls this studying "examples of excellence," and asks students to
say what makes a piece of work so good before they make their own
([Berger, "Fostering an Ethic of Excellence," 2006](https://jaymctighe.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/04/Ron-Berger-Article.pdf),
gathered in [curriculum research note 06](../research/curriculum/06-instructional-design.md)).

## The four human-shaped statements

1. I do not have time to read books in my life. I need something that
   fits my way of existing that will still let me enjoy great novels and
   non-fiction every day.
2. I lack inspiration for what to do with my child to entertain them
   without screens. I need something that will constantly suggest new
   things to do that cater to both of our interests.
3. I read a lot of news and "takes" from people online and I even see
   newsworthy things out in the real world, and I would love a way to
   chronicle it all into a cohesive narrative, an understanding of how I
   see things to remind myself why I believe what I believe.
4. I want an easy way to align my political beliefs with who and what
   I'm voting for when I get my ballot or vote in person.

## The four computer-shaped statements

1. I want to measure the amount of negative sentiment online for any
   given public figure.
2. I want to find the best price for my grocery list on delivery apps so
   that I can comparison shop and combine trips for the cheapest (and
   least convenient) trips for the delivery drivers.
3. I want to categorize every conversation I have and chronicle them as a
   way of measuring my impact on others.
4. I want to map socioeconomic data by neighborhood so that I can use it
   to choose the optimal route for Halloween trick or treating to ensure
   high-end candy.

## Study them, write yours, trade it

One round of write, play, publish, in this order.

1. **Play: study the models.** With the four human-shaped statements in
   front of you, write down everything you notice that makes them strong
   enough to build from. Look at how each one starts, whose life it is
   about, what a better day looks like in it, and what it leaves out.
   Then look at the four computer-shaped ones and write down what they
   have in common with each other. If you are in a cohort, do this with
   your trio and keep one shared list.
2. **Write: rewrite your own.** Take the stronger of your two candidate
   problems (from stage 00) and rewrite it so it has everything on your
   list. Then write one sentence for the computer-shaped version of the
   same wish, the one that measures or ranks or optimizes, so you can see
   what your app will have to steer away from.
3. **Publish: trade it for critique.** Read your statement to your trio,
   or to one person you trust, and ask for their response. Ron Berger's
   rules for critique
   ([Berger, "Fostering an Ethic of Excellence"](https://jaymctighe.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/04/Ron-Berger-Article.pdf))
   are short: "Be kind; be specific; be helpful," and
   suggestions go in as questions: "Have you considered...?" Then revise
   once, and keep both versions in your note.

## What the research noticed

After you have made your own list, compare it with this one, from
[curriculum research note 06](../research/curriculum/06-instructional-design.md)
(round 2). Add whatever is on yours and missing here.

- Each of the four human-shaped statements names a real situation in the
  first person ("I do not have time," "I lack inspiration," "I read a
  lot").
- Each says what a better life would look like ("still let me enjoy great
  novels and non-fiction every day"), not what the app would do.
- None of them names a technology.
- The computer-shaped statements share their verbs: measure, find the
  best price, categorize, map.

The closing lines of my piece say the difference in one breath:

> Human-shaped problems see the world as interrelated stories to be
> told, experienced, and added to.
>
> Computer-shaped problems see the world as disparate data points to be
> aggregated, quantified, and leveraged.

**Go deeper.** Evgeny Morozov has a name for the computer-shaped habit,
*solutionism*: recasting complex human situations as problems with neat,
computable answers
([his interview with Public Books](https://www.publicbooks.org/the-folly-of-technological-solutionism-an-interview-with-evgeny-morozov/),
gathered in [curriculum research note 02](../research/curriculum/02-landscape.md)).
Read it after you have written your own statement, and check your
statement against it.
